Anima Anandkumar on Latent Space: AI for the physical world

Latent Space, AI-for-science track. Hosts: Brandon (RNA therapeutics with AI at Atomic AI) and RJ Honakee (CTO and founder of Miraomics, spatial transcriptomics). Guest: Anima Anandkumar, Bren Professor of mathematics and computer science at Caltech, formerly head of AI research at NVIDIA and founding member of the AWS cloud AI team. 1h 23m.

The thesis: ideas are cheap, verification is the bottleneck

Anandkumar has worked in AI for more than two decades, starting in the theory of probabilistic models before deep learning, then scaling AI in industry at AWS and NVIDIA, and now back at Caltech. Her framing of "AI for science" splits the problem in two. Language models can generate hypotheses — but "ideas are not enough." The bottleneck is testing and verifying that ideas work in the real world. So her program has two arms. The first arm keeps the physics correct: neural operators, models that respect the structure of physical systems. The second arm keeps the claims correct: formal verification in Lean, which is where TorchLean lives. TorchLean lets you write neural networks in a PyTorch-like style inside Lean, so the same network can be executed and formally reasoned about — certified robustness bounds (she names CROWN as one implemented algorithm family), sensitivity analysis ("if the inputs are perturbed by a certain amount, how much is the output perturbed"), and finite-precision effects. Her motivating examples are control loops: drones, nuclear reactors — systems where you want a bound before you deploy. She is direct about the current limits: Lean is CPU-based, getting it onto GPUs "has a lot of nuances," and scaling verification to very large networks like modern transformers still requires a lot of work. What exists today is the framework.

Why physics-informed neural networks fail, and what neural operators change

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) write down a PDE residual as a loss and hope optimization finds a solution from scratch, per equation instance. Anandkumar's diagnosis: for time-dependent, potentially turbulent problems the optimization landscape is hopeless — fine-scale effects dominate and "this is not an optimization landscape we can have any handle on." Neural operators are her answer: use data. Train on many solved instances of an equation family (real observations or simulations), then at test time the model produces solutions directly, with physics constraints available as extra guidance rather than as the sole signal. The key conceptual jump: standard neural networks map fixed-size inputs to fixed-size outputs; neural operators model input and output as continuous functions that can be discretized at any resolution. You can train at one resolution and ask for outputs at another ("zero-shot super-resolution" — with the honest caveat that going above training resolution is regularized guessing unless you add physics constraints, e.g. PDE or conservation losses enforced at the finer scale).

Fourier layers, and why Fourier

The Fourier neural operator does its main mixing in frequency space: FFT, act on a truncated set of modes, inverse FFT, plus pointwise nonlinearities, residual connections, and channel lifting between layers. Her argument for it is a trade-off: solutions of PDEs are non-local (a derivative is local, but its inverse — integration — is global), transformers capture globality at quadratic cost, and Fourier gives global coupling at quasi-linear cost. Crucially, this is not classical spectral methods reborn: numerical methods represent the signal linearly in a Fourier basis and need very fine discretization; FNO lifts the signal to a high-dimensional latent space and interleaves nonlinearities, so the network learns the right basis rather than being stuck with sines. Her own undergraduate thesis, more than two decades ago, was on the fractional Fourier transform — the old math returns, but only as a component inside a learned system.

The scaling argument against transformers for physics

Two constraints separate physical data from language. First, data volume: her weather model trained on about 50,000 global samples (the host later says 58,000; both figures appear in the conversation) — nothing like a web-scale text corpus, so inductive bias must fill the gap. Second, computational scale: language is one-dimensional, and context lengths in the millions already strain transformers. Industrial-scale physics is 3D plus time at hundreds to a thousand grid points per dimension — hundreds of billions to a trillion tokens of context. "Forget ever having a transformer for anything of this scale; all of the world's compute will not be enough." When a host pushes back with video models, she counters that generative video is optimized to "look good" at low resolution, while simulation must be precise at high resolution — you cannot throw away detail without losing the phenomenon (fluid dynamics, plasma, material deformation).

Weather: the 2021 bet that changed the field

In 2021 the team picked global weather because the data was open: ERA5 reanalysis from ECMWF, the European weather agency — historical observations assimilated with physics solvers into a global gridded record at 0.25° resolution (she quotes the grid as roughly 700 × a-few-thousand points). Weather scientists warned them off: decades of careful bottom-up physics-based forecasting would not fall to AI. The result (FourCastNet) matched traditional models on accuracy and ran tens of thousands of times faster — a supercomputer workload on a single consumer-grade GPU. The model trains autoregressively: given the current global state (wind, humidity, and so on), predict six hours ahead; roll out for longer horizons, with only light multi-step fine-tuning. She credits open-sourcing FourCastNet permissively as the reason weather agencies and companies could build on it, and notes DeepMind, Huawei and others followed a year later. Since fall 2023 the model has run publicly at ECMWF. During Hurricane Lee, FourCastNet correctly called the landfall several days earlier than the standard forecasting models. Her broader point: AI forecasting democratizes capability — small agencies in the global south get fidelity that only the biggest centers could previously afford.

One model for weather and climate: assume a sphere

Short-term weather and long-term climate were historically separate model families. Her position: "there's only one Earth" — a foundation model should do both. The blocker is stability under long rollouts. Architectures that treat the world as a flat rectangle (standard projections) blow up when rolled out for months to a year; FourCastNet 3 bakes in spherical geometry (spherical harmonics — "the Fourier saves us," natural on the globe, unlike a Mercator projection where "Greenland becomes huge"), and the same model then stays stable in long rollouts. She says the Allen AI Institute has built climate emulators on this neural operator architecture and calls it the only architecture that works as an AI climate emulator, because climate requires the globe assumption. Climate projections come from ensembles: many trajectories with perturbed initial conditions, each member individually respecting the physics (constraints are checked per member, not on the ensemble average). Ensembles are also where the speed advantage compounds: traditional centers can barely afford one high-resolution run; an AI model that is tens of thousands of times faster can afford large ensembles, which is exactly what probabilistic risk assessment of hurricanes needs. Open problems she flags honestly: enforcing physical constraints during very long rollouts without washing out fine detail is unsolved and under active research, and poles remain the hard part of the sphere.

The physical world is forgiving

A surprise she keeps encountering: rare events like hurricanes are predicted well despite scarce examples, because extreme physical events have a specific physical signature — "extreme, but in a very specific way." Nature has a lot of latent structure that data-driven models uncover and traditional numerical methods (built for worst-case correctness) do not exploit. Fusion is her sharpest example: a plasma digital twin for a tokamak, trained with the UK Atomic Energy Authority on barely a few thousand samples, predicts disruption events well and runs a million times faster than traditional magnetohydrodynamics simulation. (Disruption: the plasma collapses into a beam that can damage the containment vessel — the reason reactors shut down early and a core obstacle to sustainable fusion.) The next step is closing the loop: design the control and the simulation together to prevent disruptions, with work extending to stellarators and other reactor geometries. She frames AI as the risk-taker's tool: in the digital twin you can explore reactor designs that would be too expensive or risky to try physically.

From carbon to chips: one latent-space trick everywhere

The same operator-learning machinery shows up across domains: modeling CO2 sequestration underground (plume spread and pressure build-up over decades, much faster than traditional simulation); aerodynamics over arbitrary geometries, where a car is deformed to a donut, physics is modeled on the donut in latent space, and mapped back — one model generalizing across shapes; and inverse design, where instead of simulating a given design you ask for the best one: masks for inverse lithography, gates for quantum dots, nonlinear photonics. Collaborators struggled to find these designs manually; AI finds efficient designs with the forward simulation in the loop as a physics guardrail. Her stated future: a foundation model for physics — multi-physics, coupled phenomena, both simulation and design. Complete transfer to unseen physics is impossible ("we will never have data about a new discovery"), but modular curricula work: teach heat propagation and material stretching separately, then fine-tune the coupled thermo-mechanical problem with far fewer samples. Evidence of that shows up across several of her papers.

Career arc, the UN seat, and the call to action

Her career reads as a full circle: theory (tensor methods for probabilistic models like latent Dirichlet allocation, when deep learning did not yet work), then scale (AWS, NVIDIA — "theory should not be a constraint"), and now principled methods again, because purely data-driven approaches are saturating and the physical world demands extrapolation — her definition of discovery. On the UN Scientific Advisory Board, her agenda is to keep scientists in the room: regulatory frameworks tend to equate AI with language models, and one-size-fits-all rules written for chatbots would strangle AI for science, which she argues has a different risk profile and enormous benefit (weather, agriculture, energy). Asked for the one bottleneck she would remove: compute, especially for research. Her call to action: go play with the open-source neural operator library (part of the PyTorch ecosystem, with documented architectures and recipes) — and think of AI for science as more than language models and agents: "until we have AI that fully understands the physical world not just as symbols but as one that can simulate and design and control, there's a big piece missing."

Numbers that anchor the episode: ~50,000 training samples for global weather (host: 58,000) · 0.25° ERA5 grid, ~700 × a-few-thousand points · 6-hour autoregressive step · tens of thousands of times faster than supercomputer NWP · ensembles of a few tens of members · in ECMWF's public suite since fall 2023 · Hurricane Lee landfall called days earlier · fusion digital twin: a few thousand samples, a million times faster than MHD simulation · 1000³ grid + time ≈ hundreds of billions to a trillion context tokens — the anti-transformer argument.

Neural Operator: Learning Maps Between Function Spaces (arXiv 2108.08481)

Kovachki, Li, Liu, Azizzadenesheli, Bhattacharya, Stuart, Anandkumar — JMLR. 97 pages.

The problem: networks are tied to grids; science needs maps between functions

Standard neural networks map between finite-dimensional vector spaces, so they are tied to one fixed grid. Many scientific tasks instead need a map between functions — for example, from a PDE's coefficient function to its solution function. A grid-bound model may not generalize to a different discretization and may need a new architecture per resolution. The paper defines neural operators: networks whose layers are linear integral operators composed with pointwise nonlinear activations, acting directly on function spaces. The target is an operator G† : A → U between Banach spaces of functions, learned from N input–output pairs drawn i.i.d. from a measure μ, by empirical risk minimization in the L²-μ Bochner norm.

Two properties are proved, and the authors claim neural operators are the only known model class with both. Discretization invariance: a fixed-parameter model must accept any input point set, allow output evaluation at any point, and converge to a continuum operator as the discretization refines. Graph networks and transformers accept any resolution but fail the convergence requirement; CNNs fail because their receptive field changes with the grid and their parameter count diverges in the continuum limit. Universal approximation: neural operators approximate any continuous operator between the studied spaces. Plain NNs have neither property; interpolation-based models are invariant but not universal; DeepONets are universal but, in basic form, not discretization invariant.

How a neural operator computes

A local map P lifts the input pointwise to a higher-dimensional channel vector. Then T layers (typically 4) each compute vt+1(x) = σ(W vt(x) + ∫D κ(t)(x,y) vt(y) dν(y) + b(x)) — a kernel integral operator (the function-space analog of a weight matrix), plus a local linear ResNet-style term W, a bias function, and a pointwise activation. A local map Q projects back. Because everything is defined on functions, one parameter set serves all discretizations; total error splits into discretization error (refine the grid) plus approximation error (add parameters). Kernel variants may depend on the input a (useful for Darcy flow) or on v itself, which makes the operator nonlinear. Appending the coordinate x (and optionally smoothed inputs and gradients) to the channels is a standard trick.

Naive quadrature costs O(J²) in the number of grid points, so the paper gives four efficient parameterizations. The Graph Neural Operator (GNO) truncates the integral to a ball and Nyström-subsamples J′ points via message passing, cost O(J J′). The Low-rank Neural Operator (LNO) writes the kernel as a learned rank-r sum of products, cost O(J). The Multipole Graph Neural Operator (MGNO) mimics the fast multipole method — sparse short range, low-rank long range on nested coarser grids, computed as a V-cycle — also O(J). The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) assumes κ(x,y) = κ(x−y), so by the convolution theorem the layer is u = F⁻¹(R · F(v)): the kernel is parameterized directly by its truncated Fourier coefficients, O(J log J) with the FFT, typically keeping kmax = 12 modes per dimension. Two subsumption results place rivals inside the framework: a neural operator with a pointwise-parameterized first kernel and discretized integrals is exactly a DeepONet (and that parameterization breaks discretization invariance; their DeepONet-Operator fix restores it), and single-headed transformer attention is a special case of the nonlinear kernel layer under Monte Carlo discretization. DeepONet-style models are linear approximation, bounded by Kolmogorov n-widths that decay slowly for advection-dominated PDEs; the full neural operator is nonlinear approximation and empirically wins at equal parameter counts.

Theory, stated honestly

Theorem 8: on domains where A and U embed continuously into continuous functions, n-layer neural operators are discretization invariant in the precise sense (Riemann-sum instantiations converge uniformly on compact sets). Theorem 11: for Lp, Sobolev Wm,p, or continuous-function spaces on Lipschitz domains and continuous G†, for any compact K and ε ≤ 1 there is a neural operator within ε uniformly on K; in Hilbert-space targets it can be chosen bounded by 4M. Theorems 12–14 extend to Cm inputs (derivatives as extra inputs) and to density in the L²-μ Bochner norm. The authors' own caveats: the analyzed class simplifies the practical one (P, Q identity, W zero); the proofs reduce to linear approximation, so they do not explain the observed benefit of nonlinearity or depth; no rates in parameter count or data size; parameter growth can be super-exponential in general; and the general theory does not cover FNO (separate FNO universality results exist for Hilbert spaces).

Limitations and takeaway

Stated limits: the error plateaus — the method is resolution-invariant, not resolution-convergent; the fast FNO needs uniform grids and periodic-type geometry (the local W term plus padding handles non-periodic boundaries; non-uniform geometry needs extra machinery); full O(J²) integration still beats FNO by about 40% in accuracy at much greater cost; FNO struggles with discontinuous outputs (advection), and the known CNN-branch fix breaks discretization invariance. At small parameter budgets (~10³) kernel-network methods beat FNO (0.070–0.075 vs 0.200 on Darcy); FNO wins at 10⁵–10⁶ (0.015–0.020). The takeaway: learn the operator, not a grid-to-grid map — one trained model transfers across meshes and resolutions and drops into downstream tasks like Bayesian inversion with orders-of-magnitude speedups.

Fourier Neural Operator for Parametric PDEs (arXiv 2010.08895)

Li, Kovachki, Azizzadenesheli, Liu, Bhattacharya, Stuart, Anandkumar — ICLR 2021. 16 pages.

The problem: solvers are slow, prior neural methods are stuck

The audience is anyone who must solve PDEs many times over — airfoil design, molecular dynamics, micro-mechanics, turbulence — where an inverse problem can need thousands of forward evaluations. Traditional finite-element and finite-difference solvers trade speed against grid fineness. Prior neural approaches split into two camps with complementary flaws: finite-dimensional operators (CNN surrogates) are mesh-dependent and cannot query new points; Neural-FEM methods (PINNs, Deep Ritz) are mesh-independent but model one PDE instance — every new coefficient means retraining — and need the PDE known. Neural operators learn the map between function spaces from data alone, but until this paper their integral kernels were too expensive to compete.

The trick: parameterize the kernel in Fourier space

Each layer updates vt+1(x) = σ(W vt(x) + (K vt)(x)). Restrict the kernel to κ(x−y): K becomes a convolution, and by the convolution theorem K v = F⁻¹(F(κ) · F(v)). So a Fourier layer is: FFT; keep only the low modes up to kmax per dimension (implemented as the "corners" of the spectrum); multiply by a learned complex weight tensor R of shape kmax × dv × dv with conjugate symmetry (the kernel is real); inverse FFT; add the parallel pointwise path W vt; apply the activation. They tried making R a function of the wavenumber (linear worked equally, a neural net worse); direct weights won. Complexity is quasi-linear — O(n log n) for the FFT — but the FFT requires a uniform grid. Because parameters live in Fourier space, evaluating at any resolution just projects onto e2πi⟨x,k⟩, defined everywhere: that yields discretization invariance, consistent error at any resolution, and zero-shot super-resolution — train coarse, evaluate fine, no fine training data.

The three benchmarks, with the numbers

Setup: four Fourier layers, ReLU, batch norm; N = 1000 train / 200 test; Adam 500 epochs, lr 0.001 halved every 100 epochs; kmax,j = 16, dv = 64 (1-d) or kmax,j = 12, dv = 32 (2-d); one 16 GB V100. In practice 12 modes per dimension sufficed everywhere. Burgers' equation (1-d, ν = 0.1, solved at 2¹³ = 8192 and subsampled): FNO stays flat at 0.0139–0.0160 from s = 256 to 8192, vs LNO ≈0.019–0.022, MGNO 0.0243–0.0374, PCANN ≈0.039, GNO 0.0555–0.0699, and an FCN growing from 0.0958 to 0.3238 with resolution — about 30% below the best prior method. Limitations and takeaway

Stated caveats: the ν = 10⁻⁴ setting needed 10,000 solver-generated training pairs, and for harder PDEs even a few samples can be very expensive (combining with numerical solvers is left as future work); the FFT needs a uniform grid; existence of minimizers of the operator-learning objective in infinite dimensions is open. The takeaway: global linear spectral convolutions plus local nonlinear activations give a resolution-invariant surrogate that is faster and more accurate than every learning-based baseline tested — and the authors suggest the discretization-invariance argument extends beyond PDEs to vision, where images are functions on 2-d domains.

FourCastNet 3: probabilistic ML weather forecasting at scale (arXiv 2507.12144)

NVIDIA + collaborators. 46 pages.

The problem: fast models blur or fizz; slow models cost a fortune

FourCastNet 3 (Bonev, Kurth and colleagues at NVIDIA, LBNL, UC Berkeley, Caltech; v2, 18 July 2025) targets probabilistic weather forecasting from medium range out to subseasonal timescales. Traditional numerical weather prediction is accurate but so expensive that ensemble sizes stay small. ML models are fast but flawed in characteristic ways: deterministic ones blur toward an ensemble mean; probabilistic ones like GenCast and AIFS-CRPS accumulate spurious small-scale noise — a pattern that, in classical NWP, can precede numerical blow-up. And most leading ML weather models borrow architectures built for other tasks (transformers, graph nets) instead of respecting the sphere.

Architecture: a spherical neural operator with two kinds of convolution

FCN3 is framed as a hidden Markov model: given the 0.25° atmospheric state at time t, predict t+6h. Stochasticity enters through conditioning noise drawn from 8 spherical Gaussian diffusion processes at different length scales (the Palmer stochastic-parametrization construction; spatial parameters kT from 3.08×10⁻⁵ to 5.05×10⁻¹) that evolve in time — each fixed noise seed defines one coherent ensemble member, so an ensemble is many forward passes with different seeds, with no iterative denoising. The processor alternates two convolution types: global convolutions parameterized spectrally via the spherical harmonic transform (the SFNO idea — the podcast's "assume a sphere," analogous to pseudo-spectral NWP) and local anisotropic discrete-continuous (DISCO) convolutions with a Morlet-wavelet filter basis (analogous to finite differencing; suited to tilted isentropic flow and topographic blocking), at a ratio of 4 local blocks per global block. Blocks adapt ConvNeXt but deliberately drop layer normalization — absolute magnitudes carry physical meaning — using careful initialization and learnable per-channel scaling instead. Three separate encoders (atmospheric, surface, auxiliary+noise) downsample 721×1440 to a 360×720 Gaussian grid without mixing channels of very different spectra (677 latent channels total); the decoder upsamples with bilinear spherical interpolation to avoid checkerboards; water channels pass through a positivity-enforcing "softclamp" spline. Unusually, FCN3 predicts the full next state rather than a tendency — residual prediction locked the model into Euler-like stepping and amplified high-frequency artifacts over rollouts. Total: 710,867,670 parameters.

Training: CRPS in both physical and spectral space, at serious scale

The objective is a composite CRPS (continuous ranked probability score — a proper scoring rule minimized only when the predicted distribution matches the truth): a pointwise ensemble-CRPS integrated over the sphere, plus a spectral CRPS on every spherical harmonic coefficient — because pointwise CRPS ignores spatial correlation (a spatially scrambled but marginally correct ensemble would score perfectly). NeuralGCM had a similar spectral idea but low-pass filtered it. The CRPS variant is scheduled: pre-training uses the biased estimator with 16 ensemble members (the "fair" unbiased CRPS can drive one member unbounded early — if one member hits the truth exactly, fair CRPS scores zero regardless of the others), then later stages switch to fair CRPS with 2 and 4 members. Data: ERA5 at 0.25°, 72 variables (7 surface fields + 5 atmospheric fields at 13 pressure levels), 39.5 TB; train 1980–2016, test 2017, metrics on out-of-sample 2020. Compute: stage 1 on 1024 H100s for 78 hours (208,320 steps, batch 16, ensemble 16, bf16, 1.34 s/step); stage 2 on 512 A100s for 15 hours (4-step rollouts); fine-tuning on 256 H100s for 8 hours (8-step rollouts, antithetic noise centering). One model instance exceeds 80 GB of GPU memory, so they built Makani (open source): the sphere itself is decomposed across GPUs — distributed spherical harmonic transforms, distributed DISCO convolutions with custom CUDA kernels, a distributed CRPS loss — combined with ensemble and batch parallelism to reach 1024+ GPUs.

Results: IFS-ENS beaten, GenCast matched, spectra intact for 60 days

On 12-hourly 2020 initial conditions with 50-member ensembles, FCN3 beats ECMWF's IFS-ENS on CRPS, ensemble-mean RMSE and anomaly correlation, and nearly matches GenCast — matching or slightly beating it on 15 of 16 scored variables at short leads, with 2 m temperature the exception. (Fairness notes the authors themselves make: GenCast is a 12-hourly model trained through 2019 vs FCN3's 6-hourly model trained through 2016; the longer timestep halves error accumulation.) Speed: a 15-day forecast in 60 seconds on one H100, vs 8 minutes for GenCast on a TPU v5 at half the temporal resolution and about an hour for IFS on 96 AMD Epyc CPUs at 9 km — roughly 8× and 60×, "barring the differences in hardware and resolution" as the paper hedges. A 60-day forecast takes under 4 minutes on one GPU. Calibration: spread-skill ratios near 1 (slightly overdispersive to 24 h, underdispersive from 24–200 h), rank histograms confirming, with a small 850 hPa cold bias. The headline is spectral fidelity: angular power spectral density stays within −0.2 to +0.2 relative error of ERA5 even at high wavenumbers, reproduces ERA5's T639 spectral cutoff, and remains accurate at 1440 hours — 60 days, 240 autoregressive steps — while GraphCast and NeuralGCM blur and GenCast and AIFS-CRPS accumulate noise. A single deterministic member also beats the IFS single-member forecast on RMSE and ACC. Extreme-event evidence is one case study: storm Dennis over Ireland and the British Isles, initialized 2020-02-11, with the figure caption citing prediction 5 days ahead — the paper contains no tropical-cyclone calibration tables (the podcast's Hurricane Lee story belongs to earlier FourCastNet deployments at ECMWF).

Limitations and takeaway

Stated limits: CRPS and RMSE are gameable, incomplete metrics; FCN3 carries model uncertainty only — no initial-condition perturbations (adding bred vectors might fix early-lead underdispersion); baselines were scored against ARCO-ERA5, which differs slightly from official ERA5; precipitation is absent (planned as a diagnostic). Two paper-internal discrepancies the summary preserves: Table 2 lists 8 local + 2 global processor blocks while a figure caption says 8 total, and the stage-2 sample count appears as both 55,460 and 55,400. Takeaway: geometry-respecting convolutions plus a dual-domain CRPS objective deliver GenCast-class skill at a fraction of the cost, with the spectral fidelity and 60-day stability that subseasonal large-ensemble forecasting needs. Code is open: Makani (github.com/NVIDIA/makani, Apache 2.0) and torch-harmonics (github.com/NVIDIA/torch-harmonics, BSD-3).

TorchLean: Formalizing Neural Networks in Lean (arXiv 2602.22631)

Robert Joseph George, Jennifer Cruden (Caltech), Will Adkisson (WashU St. Louis), Xiangru Zhong, Huan Zhang (UIUC), Anima Anandkumar (Caltech). v2, cs.MS, 24 May 2026. 55 pages. Read in full for this report.

The problem: you verify one artifact and deploy another

Neural networks increasingly sit in pipelines where mistakes have physical cost, and a whole verification ecosystem exists to bound their behavior — solver-based tools (Reluplex, Marabou) and bound-propagation methods (IBP, CROWN, DeepPoly). But verifiers typically consume an exported surrogate of the model: TorchScript, ONNX, FX, or a custom IR. The paper names four concrete failure modes of that pipeline. (1) Export boundaries create semantic drift: trace-based export misses control flow; ONNX operator meaning depends on opset versions; the exported graph may reflect only what one run executed. (2) Floating point invalidates idealized guarantees: verification is usually stated over real arithmetic, deployment is IEEE-754 with rounding, NaNs, signed zeros and backend-specific algorithm choices — and prior work (Jia & Rinard 2020, and 2025 follow-ups) shows numerical error can refute robustness claims proved over the reals. (3) Certification stops short of the executed workflow — e.g. PINNs need certificates about derivatives, not just values. (4) Real ML bugs live at semantic boundaries: wrong shapes, silent broadcasts, dtype conversions, padding conventions, train/eval state, mask mistakes. A robustness certificate for the wrong padding convention is still a certificate — just not for your network.

The idea: one graph, one meaning

TorchLean makes the network definition the single semantic reference point, inside Lean 4 (a programming language and theorem prover with a small trusted kernel). Programs are written in a PyTorch-style API — typed tensors, layers, objectives, optimizers, data loaders, training loops — and lower to a shared operator-tagged SSA/DAG computation-graph IR. Every node carries an operator tag (linear, relu, conv2d, attention, ...) with a typed signature and a mathematical denotation. The same graph object is consumed by the executor, by reverse-mode autodiff, by IBP/CROWN bound propagation, and by certificate checkers. There is no export step to drift across. The same syntax can be interpreted over different scalar domains: exact reals for proofs, Float32/IEEE32Exec for execution, intervals or affine forms for verification.

What is actually proved

Shapes are part of tensor types (a tensor is an inductive, shape-indexed total index function), so ill-shaped programs do not typecheck and broadcasting is an explicit typed operation, not a silent runtime convention. The headline theorem (Theorem 2.1) is graph-parametric reverse-mode AD correctness: for any well-typed SSA/DAG graph over the reals satisfying local per-operator derivative-correctness hypotheses, backprop computes the adjoint Fréchet derivative of the graph's denotation — with a pointwise variant for non-smooth points like ReLU at 0, where the executable convention is deterministic. The paper's own scope table is refreshingly explicit about status: shared IR semantics (kernel-checked definitions), reverse-mode AD (proved), compilation of the supported fragment (proved; full compiler correctness future work), IBP/CROWN transfer rules (proved subset, conservative fallbacks elsewhere), certificate checking (checked, with proved core), external solvers like α/β-CROWN (untrusted producers), Float32 internal refinement IEEE32Exec ↔ rounded-real FP32 model (proved on finite executions, excluding NaN/Inf/overflow), and the link from deployed hardware Float32 to IEEE32Exec (assumed / external — the "hardware gap").

Floating point as a first-class semantics

Lean's built-in floats are opaque to the kernel, so TorchLean builds its own: IEEE32Exec, a Lean-defined bit-level model of IEEE-754 binary32 including signed zeros, subnormals, NaN/Inf and rounding; FP32/NF rounded-real proof models with compositional error envelopes (in the spirit of Coq's Flocq); Float32 endpoint interval arithmetic with proved soundness; and an explicit Arb/FLINT oracle boundary for rigorous transcendental enclosures. Stress tests justify the design: endpoint evaluation is not a reliable enclosure mechanism for transcendentals; directed rounding matters (a ties-to-even addition enclosable by IEEE32Exec directed endpoints can collapse under naive runtime Float32); signed-zero encodings can force principled interval widening.

Verification: producers make bounds, Lean checks them

Native IBP and CROWN/LiRPA-style affine propagation run directly on the shared IR, with soundness by induction over the same topological order that defines evaluation. When more tightness is needed, TorchLean switches to a certificate/checker architecture: external solvers (e.g. α/β-CROWN on a GPU) are untrusted producers; Lean checks the finite artifact — shapes, operator tags, topological order, per-node affine bounds, α relaxation parameters, optional β phase vectors — and the theorem shape is checkCert(G, Φ, C) = true ⇒ Φ([[G]]). CUDA kernels get the same treatment on the runtime side: every native call is attached to an operator tag whose meaning is already fixed by the shared semantics, with the conformance statement nativeτ(x) ≈ [[τ]]Float32(x) recorded as a tested runtime assumption, not a proof. "CUDA provides an execution backend, not a second semantics."

Modern-ML surface and theory appendices

Beyond feed-forward classifiers, the formal surface includes attention with masks as part of the operator semantics, FlashAttention specified by equality to masked scaled-dot-product attention (fused kernels relate to the denotation by theorem, not by trust), Mamba/state-space layers as finite recurrences, diffusion noising/denoising transitions, Markov-kernel MDP semantics and RL returns/Bellman operators, and self-supervised objectives (MAE, JEPA-style predictive views, variance/covariance anti-collapse losses). The appendices mechanize a real-valued ReLU universal approximation theorem on compact sets (Theorem F.1), an exact-hull interval specification for Float32 semantics (F.2), and a three-term error decomposition for Float32-executed MLPs: total error ≤ εUAT + εparam + εexec (F.4) — deliberately weaker than a full float interval-universal-approximation theorem, and stated so.

Honest limitations

The authors are clear: TorchLean is semantic infrastructure, not a throughput-optimized training stack. The verified fragment is smaller than PyTorch's surface; extending it means adding operator semantics, derivative rules, abstract transformers and finite-precision models per primitive. Native CUDA stays outside the kernel. The full α/β-CROWN optimizer (parameter optimization, branch-and-bound) is not reimplemented in Lean — external solvers stay untrusted producers. The hardware-Float32-to-IEEE32Exec refinement is target-specific and not discharged. Related work is positioned against Isabelle/HOL network verification, SciLean (Lean scientific computing), TensorLib, Flocq, and a 2025 Lean formalization of Hopfield networks, which TorchLean complements with its own mechanized energy-decrease and convergence results.

Trust boundaries, in the repo's own words

TRUST_BOUNDARIES.md (19 KB, read in full) defines five assurance levels — Lean theorem → executable checker → Prop-valued contract → FFI/native runtime → external producer — and demands claims name their level, "to avoid collapsing 'the command ran', 'the checker accepted this artifact', and 'Lean proved a mathematical statement' into one sentence." Notable specifics: CUDA buffers are borrowed (@&) and native ops are @[never_extract] so the compiler cannot common or delete effectful calls; atomicAdd-based reductions are documented as non-bit-deterministic, with an opt-in deterministic mode (TORCHLEAN_CUDA_DETERMINISTIC_REDUCTIONS=1) covering reductions, scatter/gather backprop, and pooling backward; FlashAttention's fused kernel is not a clone of Dao-AILab's IO-tiled algorithm and is retained for parity checks; the canonical graph lowering "proves forward semantic preservation only. It must not be cited as an autograd or backward-certificate theorem." A table tells you which float layer to cite for which claim. This file is the best short document in the repo.

Update 2026-08-26: the one-time setup below is DONE and verified — toolkit 13.1 installed, all four CUDA targets built, MLP trained on the GPU. Full story in the Feasibility tab. Only the determinism experiment itself remains.

What: the RTX 3050 (4 GB) is enough for every example model in this repo. One-time setup: install the CUDA toolkit inside WSL Ubuntu (the Windows driver 592.82 already passes the GPU through). The genuinely novel experiment: TorchLean documents that its atomicAdd reduction paths are not bit-deterministic and ships an opt-in fixed-order mode — measure the run-to-run gradient variance with and without TORCHLEAN_CUDA_DETERMINISTIC_REDUCTIONS=1, and the speed cost of bit-stable training.
